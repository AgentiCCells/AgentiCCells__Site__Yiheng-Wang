      A pastry chef who became a manager of agents

      “In a kitchen I managed recipes, standards and a team. Now I manage agents and rules. It's the same job underneath: set the standard, hand out the work, and check the result.”[I]

      At Web Summit 2026 she was the project manager of the RiskMandate team: the structure, the deadlines, the logistics (printing, merchandise, deliveries, the stand) and everyone's schedule.[B][T] She was also one of the three editors of PrompTugal.news: she decided which stories were published, set the main categories and withdrew articles when sources failed.[I]

      She reads one morning brief from her conductor and deals only with the items marked for her.[I] Dinis Cruz, who ran the team, wrote that she managed at two altitudes, direction where the work was outside her expertise and depth where she knew best, and that “she became a manager, not a micromanager”.[N1]

      “I didn't write a single line of code myself. Most of my time went on deciding what to do next and checking what the agents said they had done.”[L]

      What she learnt, as rules[I]

      1. “Start with one conductor and only talk to that one. Let it hand out the work.”

      2. “If you don't understand a technical choice, ask for the options and the reasons. Then decide.”

      3. “Don't add a rule as soon as you think of it. Talk it through with your conductor first.”

      4. Fit the model to the job. She moved lighter tasks from Opus to Sonnet to keep the cost down.

      5. Make reports fit how you work. She rebuilt her morning brief: her actions first, the most urgent at the top, each with where to go, what to reply and why.

      6. Keep a rhythm. Two stand-ups a day, at 12:00 and 18:00, before and during the summit.

      7. Ask for help early. When stuck, she asked Dinis Cruz's agents for help.
